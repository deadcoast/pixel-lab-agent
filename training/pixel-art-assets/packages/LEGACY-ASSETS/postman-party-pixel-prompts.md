# THE POSTMAN AND THE PARTY — character design + PixelLab prompts

Five characters. Western archetypes transposed, not costumed.

---

## 1. The transposition rule

Space westerns work because the **role survives and the apparatus transposes**. The gunslinger keeps the quick-draw; the gun becomes a blaster. The marshal keeps the badge; the jurisdiction becomes a moon. What breaks the trick is transposing the costume instead of the function — a cowboy hat in space is a joke, a cattle drive across three systems is a genre.

Here the transposition axis is not future tech. It is **salvage plus the Route** — the road network, the relay stations, and the fact that in a world with no working presses and no working radio, a verified message is the scarcest thing anyone can carry. So each archetype gets re-derived from that: what does this role become when information is the commodity and nothing can be manufactured, only found?

That question is what generated every character below. Where it produced something ordinary, the character got cut.

---

## 2. THE POSTMAN — the armour

The armour comes out of the mail itself. That is the whole design, and it does three jobs at once.

**The breastplate is a mailbox.** A curved section of galvanised steel cut from the domed top of a roadside mailbox, beaten flat-ish and strapped over the sternum, rivet holes still ringing the rim where the post bolts went through. Nothing else in this world has that convex tunnel curve, which is why it survives at 32 px: a rounded dome on a flat chest, in cold grey, against a body that is otherwise all cloth. Every other faction armours in flat cut leather. He is the only man wearing a curve.

**The flag is a state machine.** The small red steel semaphore flag from that same mailbox, kept, mounted on a bracket at the left shoulder. Raised when he is carrying mail, folded down when he is empty. It is a costume detail, a game-legible status readout, and a story device in one object — people on a ridge two miles off know whether he has something for them before he is close enough to shout. It is also the only red the party is allowed.

**The soft armour is the failure.** Under the plate, a jerkin of undeliverable letters — years of mail addressed to people who were already dead, compressed into blocks, lacquered hard, and sewn into a quilted liner. Tight paper stops a blade and slows a round; this is not invented, phone books have done it. The cost is what makes it worth drawing: he is armoured in the letters he could not deliver, and the only place a viewer sees it is a tear in his side where the wadded paper edge shows white.

The polished brass letter-carrier badge stays the brightest point on the character.

## 3. THE PARTY

Four companions. Names are working names — swap freely.

**TALLY — the Gunhand.** *Archetype: the gunslinger.* Nobody has made a cartridge in fifteen years, so the fast-draw fantasy is dead and the discipline that replaced it is arithmetic. She wears her remaining rounds openly across her chest where anyone can count them, because a visible nine is a deterrent and a visible one is a fight she has to talk her way out of. The empty loops are the character. She does not bluff about the count; her whole reputation is that the number on her chest is true.

**MERROW — the Wire Doctor.** *Archetype: the frontier surgeon.* The trades never separated back out. The person who could get a heart started was the person who could get a generator started, and after fifteen years the tool roll holds scalpels and soldering irons in the same canvas loops. Wears a butcher's rubber apron because both jobs are wet. The mirror reflector on his forehead runs off a hand-crank dynamo at his belt, and light he can produce at will is the rarest thing anyone in the party owns.

**KELL — Roadwise.** *Archetype: the scout.* He doesn't track animals. He tracks roads — which bridges still hold, which passes flood, which culvert a rider can sleep in. Fifteen years of his own annotations live on rolled canvas sheets carried in a fan of tubes across his back, and the party's actual asset is that they are all in his head anyway. His cloak is cut from highway signs: retroreflective vinyl strips, dead flat by day, and a blinding white flare the instant anything throws a light at him. Camouflage and countermeasure from the same salvage.

**ADA — the Reader.** *Archetype: the circuit preacher.* Most people born after the collapse cannot read, so the gospel is literacy and she is paid in food to stand in a square and read strangers' letters aloud. The circuit preacher's portable pulpit became a hinged writing desk slung flat across her back, scarred along one edge from the times it has been used as a shield. Her wire-mended spectacles are irreplaceable — nobody grinds glass any more — and she is the reason the mail means anything, because a letter nobody can read is just paper.

## 4. The system that makes them read as one party

**Everyone carries a container of information on the body.** Satchel, map tubes, writing desk, tool wrap, a visible count of rounds. It is the party's shared grammar and it is why five mismatched silhouettes still read as one unit — they are all couriers of something.

**Temperature splits the sides.** The party is cool and neutral: slate, dust brown, bone, galvanised grey, dead green. The Reapers own hot: hi-vis orange, oxblood, saturated red. In a wide shot the two sides separate before a viewer resolves a single figure.

**Polished brass is the badge and nothing else.** Tally's cartridge loops are tarnished green-black, and every other brass in the world is dead. He polishes his every day — that is a character act, not a palette rule, and it is why the badge is the brightest thing in any frame he stands in.

**The two reds are disambiguated by position.** The Postman's flag and the blackguard's neckerchief are the same red on purpose — the one thing both sides claim. At sprite scale that would be ambiguous, so: his red sits **above the shoulder line** on a spike; theirs sits **at the throat, under the chin**. Height tells you which army you're looking at, at any distance.

**Silhouette test — all five at 32 px:**

| | Reads as |
|---|---|
| Postman | convex grey dome on the chest, red spike above the shoulder |
| Tally | wide flat hat brim, narrow body, banded chest |
| Merrow | flat pale rectangle torso, hunched |
| Kell | ragged fringed noisy outline, tube fan on the back |
| Ada | hard flat rectangle behind the shoulders, upright |

**Load-bearing, don't cut:** `curved galvanised steel breastplate cut from a mailbox, domed over the chest` (P1), `empty gaps where rounds are spent` (P2), `pale rubber butcher's apron` (P3), `cloak of cut highway-sign strips` (P4), `hinged wooden writing desk slung flat across the back` (P5).

The five silhouette anchors in the table above are what has to survive, and any sprite that loses its anchor is a reroll, not a fix.

## 7. Cut, and why

Recorded so they don't get re-proposed later.

- **The Kid / apprentice courier.** Adds no silhouette the party doesn't have and reduces the Postman to a mentor, which is the wrong centre of gravity for him.
- **The Gambler / fixer.** The trade-and-rumour function is already Ada's — she goes where people gather and she is paid for information.
- **The Blacksmith.** Merrow's tool roll covers it. A separate armourer splits one good character into two thin ones.
